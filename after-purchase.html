Field Guide

In an emergency.

A quick-reference protocol for use of the kit. Read it now while it's calm — not in the moment. The order is intentional: airway and bleeding first, everything else second.

1
A–B: Airway & Breathing
⚡ Major bleeding? Skip to Section 2, return here after.
A — Airway
  1. Check & clear the airway — remove any visible obstruction.
  2. Choking?
    • Abdominal thrusts from behind.
  3. Unresponsive? Open airway:
    • Jaw-Thrust or Head-Tilt, Chin-Lift
B — Breathing
  1. No breath + no pulse?
    • CPR: 30 compressions, 2 breaths — repeat
    • Ask 911 or bystanders for an AED
    • Maintain CPR → go to Section 2
  2. No breath, has pulse?
    • 1 rescue breath every 6 seconds
    • Maintain rescue breaths → go to Section 2
  3. Breathing + pulse present? → Go to Section 2
2
C — Bleeding / Circulation
  1. Head wound?
    • ABD pads on wound · gentle pressure
  2. Neck, core, back, pelvis?
    • Pack hemostatic gauze deep · firm pressure
  3. Arm or leg?
    • ABD pads · firm pressure
    • No stop → TOURNIQUET as high as possible on limb
    • Not on a knee or elbow joint
    • Tighten until bleeding stops
    • Write the time on their skin
  4. Bleeding stopped? → Yes · Section 3  |  No · repeat 1–3
↩ Came here for urgent bleeding? Return to Section 1 — Airway.
3
D–E–G: Disability, Environment & Glucose
D — Disability (Neuro Check)
  1. Ask: name · time · location · what happened
    • Wrong / no answer → prepare for evacuation
  2. Check hands & feet — movement, sensation, warmth?
    • Any absent → minimize movement (spine/brain injury risk)
E — Environment
  1. Cold, wet, or major bleeding?
    • Remove wet clothing
    • Insulate from the ground
    • Wrap in space blanket
G — Glucose
  1. Anxious, confused, or sweating?
    • Administer glucose between teeth and cheeks
⚠ Untreated hypoglycemia can cause cardiac arrest.
4
Evacuation Criteria

Evacuate immediately if any apply:

Major bleeding or burns
Tourniquet or hemostatics used
Difficulty breathing
Confusion, agitation, anxiety
Hands / feet numb or immobile
Vitals outside normal ranges
Never be too proud to call for a helicopter and Search & Rescue.

Blister Care
  1. Pop with a sterile safety pin
  2. Leave intact skin — remove only loose/dead skin
  3. Clean with warm soapy water (if available)
  4. Clean again with an alcohol wipe
  5. Apply triple antibiotic over the blister
  6. Cover with a band-aid
  7. Cut 2+ moleskin "donuts" — place around blister, not on it. Stack to create space between blister and force causing blister.
  8. Apply Leukotape or duct tape over moleskin to secure
  9. Repeat steps 3–8 every 12–24 hrs
💡 Bonus — Blister Prevention: At first sign of a hot spot, alcohol wipe the area and apply Leukotape directly over it.
Get Trained

The kit is the tool.
Training is the skill.

A first-aid kit without training is half a kit. Tourniquets, hemostatic gauze, and rescue breathing all require practice to use safely. Before you rely on this in a real emergency, take a course — ideally before your next big trip.

Strongly recommended before relying on this kit in a real emergency: at minimum, a Wilderness First Aid (WFA) course and basic CPR / BLS certification. For longer or more remote trips, step up to WFR. For team leaders, guides, and the truly committed — WEMT.
WFA
Wilderness First Aid
~16 hours · 2 days

The entry-level wilderness medicine course. Covers patient assessment, bleeding control, environmental injuries, common backcountry emergencies, and when to evacuate. Renewable every 2 years.

Best for: weekend adventurers, parents, scout leaders, anyone with a kit.
WFR
Wilderness First Responder
~80 hours · 9–10 days

The gold-standard for outdoor leaders and serious recreational users. Hands-on scenarios, deeper anatomy, trauma management, extended patient care. The industry-standard cert for guides and outdoor educators.

Best for: trip leaders, guides, instructors, expedition members, multi-day backcountry users.
WEMT
Wilderness EMT
~150–200+ hours

Full EMT-Basic certification plus wilderness-specific modules. Combines licensed pre-hospital emergency care with the autonomy and improvisation required in remote environments. Significant time and cost investment.

Best for: professional guides, SAR team members, full-time outdoor instructors, career outdoor medicine.
Training Providers

Where to take a course.

These are the providers I trust. All are well-established, employ practicing medical professionals, and are recognized across the outdoor industry. Many run courses through partner organizations (REI, universities, climbing gyms, scout councils) so search nearby — courses are more available than you'd expect.

NOLS Wilderness Medicine Visit →
WFA WFR WEMT Nationwide
The biggest and most widely recognized wilderness medicine school in the US. Hybrid online + in-person formats available. Their certs are accepted everywhere.
SOLO Wilderness Medicine Visit →
WFA WFR WEMT NH + nationwide
The oldest wilderness medicine school in the US (founded 1976). Excellent instructors, strong scenario-based curriculum. Hosts courses across the country.
AERIE Backcountry Medicine Visit →
WFA WFR Mountain West
Missoula, MT-based. Smaller class sizes, strong reputation in the climbing and guiding community. Active in the Mountain West.
Desert Mountain Medicine Visit →
WFA WFR Mountain West
Based in the Mountain West with regular courses in Colorado, Utah, Idaho, and Wyoming. Field-heavy curriculum, instructors are practicing wilderness medics.
Wilderness Medical Associates Visit →
WFA WFR WEMT International
International reach with courses across the US, Canada, and abroad. Particularly strong in the Northeast and Canadian guiding community.
American Red Cross Visit →
CPR / BLS First Aid Nationwide
For baseline CPR and AED certification, plus a non-wilderness First Aid course. Cheap, widely available, easy to schedule. A solid floor under any wilderness training.
American Heart Association Visit →
BLS CPR / AED
AHA's BLS (Basic Life Support) course is the medical-professional standard for CPR. Find a course through their partner network — the official source for the protocols this guide references.
